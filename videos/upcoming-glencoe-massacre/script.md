# \[1:30 – 2:48\] CHAPTER 1: THE OATH DEADLINE

**\[Talking Head\]**

*(Storyteller. Set the scene.)*

To understand Glencoe, go back to 1688.

King James the Seventh, a Catholic Stuart, is driven from the throne.

William of Orange takes the crown of England and Scotland.

But in the Scottish Highlands, many clans stay loyal to the exiled
James.

They're called Jacobites. And in 1689, they rise in rebellion.

The rising fails. But the government is still afraid of the Highlands.

So in August 1691, it offers a deal.

Every Highland chief must swear an oath of loyalty to King William.

The deadline? The 1st of January, 1692.

Swear... and be pardoned.

Refuse... and face, in the government's words, "the utmost extremity of
the law."

**\[Talking Head\]**

*(Warmer. Character portrait.)*

Now meet the chief of the MacDonalds of Glencoe.

Alasdair MacIain. Old, white-haired, and by many accounts, enormously
tall.

His clan was small. Proud. Feared by its neighbors as cattle raiders.

And MacIain was stubborn.

He waited for permission from the exiled King James before swearing
anything.

That permission arrived late in December.

Which meant the old chief now had only days.

*(Beat. Re-hook.)*

And the road to that oath was about to become a trap.

# \[2:48 – 4:09\] CHAPTER 2: THE INVERARAY DELAY

**\[Talking Head\]**

*(Tense. Ticking clock.)*

In the last days of 1691, MacIain rides through the snow to Fort
William.

He offers to swear the oath to the governor, Colonel John Hill.

But Hill can't accept it. He's a soldier, not a magistrate.

The oath must be sworn before the sheriff... at Inveraray.

Dozens of miles away. Across frozen mountains. In the middle of winter.

Hill gives him a letter, asking the sheriff to receive him anyway.

And MacIain sets out again.

*(Faster. Clipped.)*

On the way, soldiers stop him and hold him. He loses precious time.

He reaches Inveraray in the first days of January.

Already too late.

And the sheriff isn't even there. Bad weather and the New Year holiday
keep him away.

For days, the old chief waits.

On the 6th of January, the sheriff finally returns.

And MacIain, reportedly in tears, swears his loyalty to King William.

*(Pause.)*

They were not.

Because his certificate was sent to Edinburgh...

And in London, someone was very glad to hear it.

# \[4:09 – 5:26\] CHAPTER 3: THE MASTER OF STAIR

**\[Talking Head\]**

*(Cold. Every line deliberate.)*

His name was Sir John Dalrymple. The Master of Stair.

He was King William's Secretary of State for Scotland. A brilliant
lawyer. A ruthless one.

And he despised the Highland clans.

Dalrymple didn't want obedience. He wanted an example.

Something so terrible it would frighten every other clan into line.

Glencoe was small. Isolated. Hated by powerful neighbors.

Including the most powerful family in the western Highlands. Clan
Campbell.

The Campbell Earl of Breadalbane had been trying to buy the clans'
loyalty with government money. And MacIain had clashed with him. Glencoe
raiders had stolen Campbell cattle for years.

And now, technically, it was outside the law.

*(Slow. Chilling.)*

In his letters, Dalrymple wrote that it would be "a proper vindication
of the public justice to extirpate that sept of thieves."

Extirpate. Wipe out. Root and branch.

On the 16th of January, King William signed the orders.

Historians still argue about how much the king really understood.

But his signature was there.

*(Lower. Conspiratorial.)*

And the plan was even darker than a simple attack.

The soldiers wouldn't storm Glencoe.

They would be invited in.

# \[5:26 – 6:51\] CHAPTER 4: 12 DAYS OF HIGHLAND HOSPITALITY

**\[Talking Head\]**

*(Softer. Almost warm. Let them feel the hospitality.)*

On the 1st of February, 1692, around 120 soldiers march into Glencoe.

Their commander is Captain Robert Campbell of Glenlyon.

Sixty years old. Deep in debt. A man who had lost his lands to drink and
gambling.

And he had a grudge against the MacDonalds, whose raiders had plundered
his estate.

But here's the twist.

Glenlyon's own niece was married to one of MacIain's sons.

He was family. A guest the clan could never refuse.

The soldiers say they only need shelter. The fort is full. They're here
to collect taxes.

And the MacDonalds do what Highlanders always did.

They open their doors.

**\[B-roll\]**

A long line of red-coated soldiers winding into the white glen. A

**\[Talking Head\]**

*(Building tension under the warmth.)*

In the Highlands, hospitality was close to sacred.

Once you had eaten under a man's roof, you were safe. You did not harm
him. Ever.

For twelve days, the soldiers live with the MacDonalds.

They share their food, their fires, their whisky.

They sleep in the MacDonalds' beds. The clan's children run between the
houses, laughing with the soldiers.

Glenlyon visits MacIain's house almost every day.

On the night of the 12th of February, he plays cards with MacIain's
sons.

And he accepts an invitation to dine with the old chief the next day.

*(Pause. Dead quiet.)*

That same evening... a letter reaches him.

**\[B-roll\]**

Soldiers and Highland children laughing by a hearth. Cards slapped down

**\[Talking Head\]**

**\[Talking Head\]**

"Grey stone of the glen, if you knew what will happen tonight... you

A warning. Disguised as a riddle.

Some understood. Most were asleep.

But most of the soldiers obeyed.

*(Pause.)*

# \[8:07 – 9:29\] CHAPTER 6: INTO THE BLIZZARD

**\[Talking Head\]**

*(Restrained. Emotional. No gore, only weight.)*

They came for MacIain first.

The old chief was getting out of bed to greet his guests. He was shot in

His wife was stripped and thrown out into the snow. She is said to have

Across the glen, the soldiers broke into the homes where they had slept.

Men were shot in their beds. Houses were set on fire.

*(Slow. Heavy.)*

But the real horror was outside.

*(Quieter. Devastated.)*

Hundreds of MacDonalds ran into the mountains. In nightclothes. Carrying

Into a blizzard.

*(Lean in.)*

And yet... the plan failed.

Another force of soldiers was meant to block the escape routes. The
storm held them back. They arrived too late.

MacIain's sons escaped into the hills. Warned, some accounts say, by a

The clan survived.

But the government had done something far worse than kill.

It had broken the oldest law the Highlands knew.

# \[9:29 – 11:07\] CHAPTER 7: MURDER UNDER TRUST

**\[Talking Head\]**

*(Measured. Building outrage.)*

News of Glencoe spread slowly. But it spread.

Pamphlets. Rumors. Fury in Edinburgh.

The government tried to keep the details quiet. For three years, it
mostly worked.

Then, in 1695, under pressure from the Scottish Parliament, the king
appointed a commission to investigate.

Its verdict was damning.

It found that Dalrymple's letters had gone far beyond the king's orders.

And the Scottish Parliament voted that the killing of the Glencoe
MacDonalds was murder.

And Scottish law had a special name for this kind of crime.

*(Slow. Let it land.)*

Murder under trust.

Killing someone who had trusted you. Who fed you. Who gave you shelter.

In Scots law, it was treated as one of the worst crimes a man could
commit... a crime treated as seriously as treason.

**\[B-roll\]**

Pamphlets fluttering off a printing press. The Scottish Parliament hall
in candlelight, packed benches. The words "MURDER UNDER TRUST" carved
into stone, letter by letter.

**\[Talking Head\]**

*(Bitter. Cold.)*

So who paid?

Dalrymple was blamed. He lost his office.

But he was never put on trial. A few years later, he was made an Earl.

King William? The inquiry was careful not to blame the king.

Glenlyon died abroad a few years later, still in debt.

Not one man was ever punished for the Massacre of Glencoe.

*(Beat. Re-hook.)*

But the Highlands did not forget.

When the Jacobite clans rose again, in 1715 and in 1745, the MacDonalds
of Glencoe marched with them.

The clan the government tried to wipe out… was still fighting fifty
years later.

And the Highlands still haven't forgotten.

## Historical Accuracy Notes

- Death toll: about 38 killed in the attack is the figure most
  historians accept; the number who died of exposure afterwards (often
  given as around 40) is uncertain.

- Glenlyon's force is usually given as about 120 men of the Earl of
  Argyll's Regiment of Foot.

- The "grey stone" warning, the Clachaig Inn "No hawkers or Campbells"
  sign, and MacIain's wife dying the next day are tradition and later
  accounts; they are presented as such.

- George R.R. Martin has said the Red Wedding drew on the Glencoe
  Massacre and the Black Dinner of 1440.

- Dalrymple quote ("extirpate that sept of thieves") and Duncanson's
  order ("put all to the sword under seventy") are from the surviving
  letters.

- The 1695 investigation was a royal commission, set up under pressure
  from the Scottish Parliament; Parliament then voted the killings
  murder.

- MacDonalds of Glencoe fought in the Jacobite risings of 1715 and

  1745.
